# Applying the Demographic Lens

## Overview

Demography is the slowest, most predictable force in strategic analysis. Population structures forecast 20–30 years out with high confidence. The demographic lens is for *establishing constraints other lenses must respect* — pension liabilities, labour supply, military manpower, urbanisation pressure, ethnic balance.

**Core principle:** *Demography is destiny — but only on demographic time scales.* It explains the trend, not the news. Use it to bound what is possible, not to forecast next quarter.

## When to Use

- Stories about ageing, fertility, migration, labour shortages, demographic "time bombs".
- Long-run forecasts (10+ years) that depend on workforce, dependents, or consumption.
- Conflict and stability analysis involving ethnic, sectarian, or generational divides.
- Pension, health-care, or housing policy.
- Urbanisation and city-system dynamics.

## The Frameworks

### Demographic transition (Notestein)

Four (or five) stages, with predictable consequences:
1. **High birth, high death** — pre-transition; small populations, low growth.
2. **High birth, falling death** — rapid population growth; dependency ratio dominated by children.
3. **Falling birth, low death** — *demographic dividend* window: working-age share peaks, dependency falls, savings rise.
4. **Low birth, low death** — population stabilises; dependency shifts to elderly.
5. **Below-replacement fertility** — population contraction; ageing accelerates.

Most analytical action is at the stage 2→3 transition (the dividend) and the stage 4→5 transition (ageing societies). Position the country first; the appropriate questions follow.

### Key indicators

- **Total fertility rate (TFR)** — children per woman. Replacement ≈ 2.1; below this, eventual decline. China ≈ 1.0, South Korea ≈ 0.7, Italy ≈ 1.2, Nigeria ≈ 5.1.
- **Population pyramid** — visual immediately tells you the regime. Pyramid (broad base) = young, growing. Column = stable. Inverted = ageing.
- **Dependency ratios** — youth (under-15) and old-age (65+) over working-age (15–64). Total dependency falls during dividend; old-age dependency rises during ageing.
- **Median age** — Niger 15, India 28, China 39, Japan 49.
- **Life expectancy** — at birth, but also healthy life expectancy, and at age 65 (matters for pensions).
- **Ethnolinguistic fractionalisation (ELF; Fearon, Alesina)** — probability two random individuals are from different groups. Higher fractionalisation correlates with weaker public-goods provision, but causation is contested.

### Migration

- **Push/pull** with **gravity** — flows scale with origin and destination size, decay with distance, network effects (existing diasporas) amplify. Diaspora networks are the single best migration predictor.
- **Migration is selective** — by age (young), education (often higher than origin average), and risk tolerance. Origin loses workforce productivity; destination gains it (with assimilation lags).
- **Refugee dynamics** — driven by violence, not poverty. Most refugees stay close (regional). Distant flows reflect specific corridors and policy.

### Youth bulges

- **Youth-bulge instability** (Goldstone, Heinsohn) — large youth share, especially male youth without economic prospects, correlates with conflict and instability. *Correlates*, not causes; mediated by institutions, opportunities, and ideology. Use as risk factor, not predictor.

### Urbanisation

- **Urbanisation curve** — most countries now follow a fast-then-slowing path; advanced economies plateau ~80%. Cities concentrate productivity, political power, and protest capacity.
- **Primate-city pattern** — single dominant city common in middle-income countries; politically destabilising (regime changes there change the country).

## Quick Indicators Reference

| Question | Look at |
|----------|---------|
| Long-run growth potential? | Working-age population trajectory, fertility, education attainment |
| Pension/health system strain? | Old-age dependency ratio, life expectancy at 65, pension formula |
| Labour-market tightness? | Working-age cohort changes, participation rates, immigration flows |
| Conflict-risk demography? | Youth bulge, ethnic fractionalisation, recent demographic shocks |
| Urbanisation pressure? | Rural-urban gap in TFR, internal migration, primate-city share |
| Migration flows? | Diaspora stock, wage gap, language overlap, policy regime |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Demographic determinism | Demography sets constraints; institutions and policy still decide outcomes |
| Linear extrapolation of fertility | Fertility has surprised forecasters in both directions; use bands |
| Confusing youth-bulge correlation with cause | Most youth-bulge countries are *not* in conflict |
| Ignoring net migration in projections | Can swamp natural change for advanced economies |
| Treating ethnicity as fixed | Identity categories are constructed, mobilised, and can change |
| One-country focus | Migration is bilateral; flows have origin and destination |
| Mistaking urbanisation rate for level | Fast-urbanising and already-urbanised need different analyses |

## Worked Example

*Headline: "Country X's labour shortage drives wages up; immigration debate intensifies"* — through the lens:

- **Position on transition:** Country X is post-stage 4 (TFR < 2.1, median age rising). Working-age cohort is contracting.
- **Indicator check:** Old-age dependency ratio trajectory; what does the next 10 years' working-age cohort look like? Already determined — those workers are alive now.
- **Policy options bounded:** Raising TFR has long lags (20+ years to labour market). Pulling more women into workforce has limits. Raising retirement age has political ceilings. **Immigration is the only short-term lever for labour supply.**
- **Migration framework:** Where would workers come from? Apply gravity and diaspora — neighbours with wage gaps and existing networks dominate.
- **Political backlash:** Immigration debate intensity correlates with pace of change, not stock. Predict: pressure for selective/temporary regimes.
- **Indicator to watch:** Net migration trends, labour-force participation of older workers, sectoral wage growth, policy moves on visa categories.
