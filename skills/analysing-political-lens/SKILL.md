# Applying the Political Lens

## Overview

The political lens reads events as moves by actors with interests, constraints, and bargaining positions. The job is not to predict the next election — it is to identify *who has agency over this outcome*, *what they want*, *what they can do*, and *what blocks them*.

**Core principle:** *Politics is about who can do what to whom, and at what cost.* Formal authority and effective power are not the same thing. Track effective power.

## When to Use

- Government action, policy announcement, or political shake-up.
- Regime stability question — protests, coups, succession, crisis.
- Coalition formation or breakdown.
- Election outcome or campaign development.
- Civil-military, centre-region, or party-faction tension.
- Any "will country X do Y" question where the answer depends on internal politics.

## The Frameworks

### Who has power?

- **Veto-player theory (Tsebelis)** — policy change requires consent of all veto players (institutional and partisan). The more veto players and the further apart their preferences, the more status-quo-biased the system. Use to assess the probability of policy change vs. continuity.
- **Selectorate theory (Bueno de Mesquita et al.)** — leaders survive by satisfying their *winning coalition*; size and composition of the coalition determine policy choices. Small coalitions → private goods, corruption, repression; large coalitions → public goods. Useful for autocracy/democracy comparisons and for predicting what regimes will sacrifice.
- **Principal-agent** — formal authority delegates to agents (ministries, militaries, oligarchs) who have their own interests. Compliance is not assumed; ask about monitoring, incentives, and exit options.
- **State capacity** (Mann's *infrastructural power*) — the capacity to actually implement decisions across the territory. High formal authority + low capacity = announcements that don't translate to action.

### Coalitions & elections

- **Median voter theorem (Downs)** — under simple-majority, single-issue, single-peaked preferences, two parties converge to the median. Useful as a baseline; deviations (multi-issue, multiparty, salience effects) explain real divergence.
- **Riker's minimum winning coalition** — coalitions tend to the smallest size that still wins, to maximise per-member share. Predicts coalition fragility when add-ons are not strictly necessary.
- **Cleavage theory (Lipset & Rokkan)** — durable political alignments form along cleavages (centre-periphery, religious-secular, urban-rural, owner-worker). New cleavages (cosmopolitan-nationalist, educated-non-educated) realign systems slowly.
- **Spatial / valence models** — voters choose based on issue position (spatial) and competence/character (valence). Scandal → valence shock; ideological shift → spatial shift. Diagnose the type.

### Regime dynamics

- **Authoritarian survival toolkit** (Svolik, Magaloni, Gandhi) — autocrats use *cooptation* (paying off threats), *repression* (raising the cost of dissent), and *legitimation* (performance, ideology, nationalism). Read regime moves as adjustments to this mix.
- **Modernisation theory and its critics** — wealth correlates with democracy but does not cause it (Przeworski: democracy is more *durable* in rich countries, not more likely to *emerge*). Don't expect economic growth alone to produce political opening.
- **Democratic backsliding** (Levitsky & Ziblatt, Bermeo) — modern erosion is gradual and legalistic: capture of referees, sidelining of rivals, rewriting rules. Watch courts, electoral commissions, media licensing, civil service.
- **Civil-military relations** (Huntington, Feaver) — *objective* control (professionalisation, autonomy) vs. *subjective* control (ideological alignment). Coup risk rises when the military's corporate interests are threatened or when civilian factions invite military intervention.

### Mass politics

- **Collective action (Olson)** — small concentrated groups organise more easily than large diffuse ones. Predicts which interests get represented (producers > consumers, incumbents > challengers, urban > rural in mobilisation, rural > urban in over-representation).
- **Resource mobilisation / political opportunity (McAdam, Tarrow)** — protests succeed when grievances meet organisational capacity and openings in the political system. Episodic protest ≠ movement.
- **Polarisation metrics** — affective (in-group/out-group warmth gap) vs. ideological (issue-position spread). Affective polarisation rising with stable ideology means status competition, not policy disagreement.

## Quick Indicators

| Question | Indicator |
|----------|-----------|
| Coalition stability? | Number of veto players, distance between them, recent defections, polling of junior partners |
| Government survival? | Approval rating trajectory, parliamentary arithmetic, no-confidence procedures, scheduled elections |
| Regime stability? | Cooptation cost (subsidy bills, civil-service hiring), repression intensity, elite cohesion (defections), economic performance |
| Protest significance? | Geographic spread, duration, sectoral participation (especially professionals, students, military families), regime response |
| Election outcome? | Polling aggregates with house-effect adjustment, base rates, fundamentals models (economy, incumbency) |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Reading formal authority as effective power | Map who actually executes, who can block |
| Treating governments as unitary actors | Open the black box: factions, ministries, agencies |
| Mirror imaging (assuming foreign leaders share your values) | Identify *their* selectorate; act in their interest model |
| Recency bias in regime-stability assessment | Most regimes that look stable are; most "fragile" labels are wrong |
| Polling overconfidence | House effects, late shifts, turnout assumptions; bands not points |
| Conflating polarisation with instability | High polarisation can coexist with high stability (US, IL); the link is not automatic |
| "The street has spoken" | Mobilised minorities can be loud and unrepresentative; check polling, not just visible protest |
| Assuming elections decide everything | Many systems centralise power between elections |

## Worked Example

*"Prime Minister X loses a coalition partner; opposition demands snap election"* — through the lens:

- **Veto players:** Coalition arithmetic — does the remaining coalition still command a majority? If not, identify which alternative coalitions are mathematically possible.
- **Riker's MWC:** A trimmed coalition is closer to minimum-winning; per-member leverage rises, but so does fragility to any single defector.
- **Selectorate:** Who in the winning coalition needs the PM? Track which ministers' portfolios depend on this PM specifically vs. survive a leadership change.
- **Civil-military:** Crisis adjacent? Watch for political moves involving security services (appointments, leaks, criminal investigations of rivals).
- **Cleavage check:** Is this a routine bargaining failure or a realignment? Routine bargaining → expect a deal; realignment → expect early elections and possibly a new party.
- **Indicators to watch:** Defection statements (named MPs), behaviour of the head of state in inviting government formation, central-bank/markets reaction (a tell on whether business-as-usual is expected).
